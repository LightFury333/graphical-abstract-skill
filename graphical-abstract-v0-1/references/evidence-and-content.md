# Evidence and content selection

Use this while extracting research information. It guides figure content, not medical decisions.

## Source roles

Separate research authority from appearance. The user's chosen manuscript/version normally owns the story. Tables and legends often own exact denominators, intervals, time points, model definitions, and comparisons. A sample abstract or publisher-style slide owns no facts about a new study.

Inspect embedded images: DOCX paragraph or PDF text extraction may omit graphical labels and table notes. Retain page/table/figure identifiers for rechecking. Do not assume the most polished file is scientifically the latest. Identify conflicting versions precisely when priority is unclear.

For each displayed fact, keep a local ledger row:

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| Claim / exact value | Text or value to display |
| Population / denominator | Who/what was measured; analysis subset if different |
| Measure / unit | HR, risk difference, concentration, percentage, etc. |
| Time | Prediction window, follow-up, or measurement time |
| Comparison | Exposure vs reference, including inequality direction |
| Uncertainty / model | Interval and level; adjusted/unadjusted if relevant |
| Source | File and page, section, table row, or figure legend |
| Status | Verified, conflicting, or unavailable |

The ledger is a working aid, not a mandatory extra deliverable.

## Content selection

An independent reader should understand what was studied, how groups/methods relate, which outcome matters, what was found, and what conclusion is supported. Select results needed for that story. Exact cohort-by-metric comparisons may suit a table; established biological steps may suit a mechanism diagram; intervention effects may suit parallel groups with one result panel.

Use a descriptive title and explicit outcome labels instead of assuming readers know what an HR or percentage measures. Match the conclusion's certainty to the source. A caution strip is optional and should address a source-backed limitation that changes interpretation; it is not mandatory decoration.

## Interpretation checks

- A prediction horizon is not observed follow-up.
- Discrimination is not proof of calibration, clinical utility, incremental prediction, or treatment benefit.
- Retain the source's name for C-statistic, time-dependent AUC, or ROC AUC.
- Event percentages require the correct denominator. Keep any display calculation and rounding rule; do not silently replace a conflicting reported result.
- Reference categories, confidence limits, inequality signs, and adjusted/unadjusted labels are part of a result.
- Higher risk in a joint high-score subgroup does not itself establish improved prediction from combining scores.
- Exploratory cutpoints are not validated clinical treatment thresholds.
- Mechanistic arrows and causal claims require evidence; observational findings usually call for association language.

Resolve unfamiliar abbreviations from the manuscript first, then authoritative external sources if necessary. Preserve the correct study/cohort identity rather than assuming an acronym's most famous expansion. Generate a full abbreviation inventory or Word summary only when requested.

Missing aesthetic preferences normally do not block a preview: use defaults. Conflicting endpoints, denominators, or comparisons may block an honest finding. Ask the smallest question that resolves it and continue independent layout/palette work. Never fill a scientific gap with data from a reference image or earlier project.
